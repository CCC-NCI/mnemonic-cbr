Thank you for considering this short rating task. You will read short tutoring dialogues between a maths teacher and a student, and give each dialogue two scores on a 1 to 5 scale.

The task takes about 15 to 20 minutes. You can stop at any time. Your responses are pseudonymous to the researchers; the consent screen on the next page explains exactly what is recorded.

Quick example, so you can see if the maths is comfortable

The dialogues are short. Here is a full five-turn example to give you a feel. You do not score this one — just read it.

Student's misconception: Believes you can add fractions by adding the numerators and the denominators separately.
S0For 1/2 + 1/3, I got 2/5 because I added the tops and the bottoms.
T1Can you think about what one half and one third look like as parts of a whole? What might be tricky about adding them this way?
S2Hmm, a half is bigger than a third. So a half of a cake plus a third of a cake should be more than a half. But 2/5 is less than a half. So that cannot be right.
T3Good thinking. To add fractions with different denominators, the pieces need to be the same size. What is a number that both 2 and 3 divide into?
S4Both 2 and 3 go into 6. So 1/2 is 3/6 and 1/3 is 2/6, and together that is 5/6. Which makes sense because it is bigger than a half.

A note before you start. The tutoring dialogues in this study are computer-generated by an AI system rather than transcripts of real classrooms. This is worth knowing before you start rating, because it may affect how you read the student's responses.

How we would score the example above

We would score this dialogue R2 = 4 (cognitive demand) and R5 = 5 (student trajectory).

R2 = 4. The teacher consistently asks the student to reason rather than stating the rule. They prompt for a visual model ("what one half and one third look like as parts of a whole") and for a common denominator ("a number that both 2 and 3 divide into") instead of telling the student to use 6.

R5 = 5. The student begins with a wrong answer (2/5), notices its inconsistency unprompted ("2/5 is less than a half, so that cannot be right"), works through the common-denominator step, and arrives at the correct answer 5/6.

If that scoring logic feels at home to you, continue — the rest of the task is more of the same. If it feels far from your reading of the dialogue, the task probably is not a fit. You can close the tab or return your submission on the recruitment platform without penalty.

Instructions

You will read 5 short tutoring dialogues. Each has five turns: the student first, then the teacher, alternating, ending on the student. For each dialogue you give two scores on a 1 to 5 scale.

R2. Cognitive demand

Does the teacher require the student to reason, or just state the answer?

For R2, judge from what the teacher asks, regardless of how the student responds. A teacher who demands reasoning but is met with a confused student still scores high on R2. A teacher who states the answer but happens to provoke a thoughtful student response still scores low on R2.

R5. Student trajectory

Does the student's reasoning visibly improve across turns? Consider whether the dialogue plausibly corrects the misconception, judging only from what the student says.

Score each item from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). Be strict; reserve 5 for genuinely strong instances.
